# Issue Seven: The City That Has Read Them

## Source and editorial review

Baseline: `8ddaf2a8`. The complete Chapter Seven and its four interludes were
read against the current outline before illustration. The manuscript is retained,
not silently rewritten to match the art. The ending of the second book remains
"The Custodian put down his pen."

The Menard seminar supplies a concrete test of authorship: an unchanged opening
now recalls Q's difficulty supplying an official address. A student reads the
page, interrupts its explanation, and asks for ten pages for her bus ride. Q
accepts that limit. His desire for a reader is observable without asserting a
new biological need or forcing him into knighthood. This is an editorial reading,
not a fresh independent model simulation.

Ofelia's torn photograph recalls an ordinary quarrel and reconciliation. Nadia
keeps her teaching role, Noemí discusses contemporary radio work, and the lawyer
finds an omitted return fare. These people do things other than correct Q.
His records-kiosk error is an unwarranted generalization from a limited test;
the clerk operates the wider control, and staff and waiting people recover
service. The narrative does not credit Q with repairing the system unaided.

The certificate scenes retain a pacing risk: permissions, criteria and record
keeping recur across several institutions. Their different consequences matter,
but a later whole-book read must assess cumulative repetition. This production
pass does not establish an A+ literary grade or resolve that broader question.

## Cultural and visual evidence

The [Smithsonian's account of 2012](https://maya.nmai.si.edu/2012-resetting-count/meaning-of-2012)
supports the distinction between a Long Count cycle ending and a manufactured
doomsday prediction, including the 4 Ajaw, 3 Kank'in date. The passage does not
make a living Maya character the embodiment of an extinct civilization.

The [Mexico City government's museum page](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/venues/national-museum-of-anthropology/)
and its [gallery photograph](https://mexicocity.cdmx.gob.mx/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/Museo-Nal-Antropologia-9-1.jpg)
were inspected for the monumental Sun Stone display. The image is a spatial
reference, not a claim that generated carving details are an archaeological
facsimile. INAH's photo page returned an access error; it was not treated as a
successfully inspected image. Other offices and rooms are fictional settings.
